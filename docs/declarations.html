AI Acknowledgement

One of the use cases for the skills map is to help students to identify how to articulate how they have used GenAI in coursework. Acknowledgements can be developed along discipline guidelines, or along institutional guidelines. Below we have examples of both.

Most journals expect the use of GenAI to be acknowledged and there is a difference between citing GenAI as a source and acknowledging it as a tool. As the output of GenAI is not stable or repeatable, it cannot be used as a source since a reader will not be able to access the source themselves. So a citation in the normal sense does not work. Some people have suggested that it can be cited like a personal communication where it would be cited in text, but not included in the references section. It is better to avoid this kind of citation and instead use a narrative description of the way that the tool was used.

Acknowledgement as a tool is done in two ways. The first is within the text, such as a methods section of a report, where an author describes how they used the tool for specific tasks. The second is a more general acknowledgement in a dedicated section. The Consortia Advancing Standards in Research Administration Information provides a summary of different journal expectations and example forms of wording. In their additional guidance the American Psychological Association (APA) provides a guide to disclosing AI situated within a range of established descriptive narrative styles for referencing tools.

When using acknowledgement sections, best practice for assessed work is to include them as an appendix or as an author note at the end of the text (before references).

Appendix with ticked items

Below is an example of a tick-box declaration of AI use. Students are fist asked if they take take responsibility for their work and are then prompted to pick different ways that they uses AI. You can use the skills map to generate an assessment-specific or dicipline-specific list of items.

This can also be developed as a survey activity on an learning management system such as Moodle. For instance teachers can make it conditional on the assessment that a student first had to complete the declaration of AI use before they were allowed submit.

Discipline-specific example

The first example is student-facing advice based on the APA guidelines above.

When you engage in putting together ideas in order to answer a question in an assessment, you are practising synthesis. This can be done through thinking and manipulating the knowledge that you have already accumulated. Alternatively it can be done by using resources from elsewhere or through brainstorming with the use of an LLM.

When doing the task yourself, you would develop a research question and use that to organise the literature and knowledge you already have to answer the assessment questions. In doing so, you develop an argument. As you answer, you would refer back to your literature to verify and cite the evidence that supports your argument.

However, people often have gaps in their knowledge and in that case they refer to the arguments that others have made or use tools to help them find the evidence to support their arguments.
